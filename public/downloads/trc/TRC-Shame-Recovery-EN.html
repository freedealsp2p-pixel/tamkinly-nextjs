Shame Recovery Worksheet

A structured worksheet for understanding shame, separating identity from behavior, and responding to yourself with greater clarity and compassion.

Safety Alert
Shame can distort the way you see yourself. You deserve support.
Shame can make a painful experience feel like a statement about who you are. A shame-based conclusion about yourself is not necessarily an accurate reflection of your identity, worth, or responsibility. You do not need to accept shame as a complete definition of yourself. Pause if this exercise increases your distress. You do not need to describe traumatic details or force yourself to continue. Return to grounding or seek appropriate support if needed.
1. Shame vs. Guilt
SHAME ("I AM bad") GUILT ("I DID something bad")
Identity-focused Behavior-focused
"I am bad." "I did something bad."
Can create global self-judgment Can focus attention on a specific behavior
Often encourages hiding or withdrawal Can support accountability and repair
"Something is wrong with me." "Something I did may need attention."
Important: Feeling shame does not automatically mean that the shame-based conclusion about yourself is true. A person can take responsibility for a behavior, mistake, or choice without defining their entire identity by it.
What difference do I notice between judging myself as a person and evaluating something I did?
Reflect on how this shift in perspective feels in your mind and body.

Understanding the Shame Response

Slow down enough to notice what activates shame without forcing yourself into unnecessary detail.

1. Identify the Shame Trigger
What situation, thought, interaction, memory, or experience seems to have activated the shame response?
Keep this brief. You do not need to describe traumatic details.
2. The Story Behind the Shame
What does the shame seem to tell you about yourself?
Write the message as it appears in your mind. You do not have to agree with it.
3. Physical Sensation
Where do you notice the shame response in your body?
Notice physical sensations without judging them. You may describe location, pressure, tension, heat, heaviness, numbness, or other sensations.

4. Earliest Memory Connection
Does this feeling remind you of an earlier experience, message, or period of your life?
Only explore this if it feels safe enough. You do not need to identify or describe a traumatic event.
Safety Note: You are allowed to stop here. You do not need to recover every memory for this exercise to be useful.

Responding to Shame

The goal is not to deny what happened. The goal is to respond to yourself without unnecessary self-attack.

3-Step Self-Compassion Exercise
STEP 1: ACKNOWLEDGE
What am I noticing right now?
Name the experience without exaggerating it or pushing it away.
STEP 2: NORMALIZE
What is understandable about having this reaction?
Consider the human context. Difficult reactions can happen when people experience stress, rejection, fear, humiliation, violation, loss, or other painful experiences.
STEP 3: SELF-KINDNESS
What would a kind, honest, and supportive response to myself sound like?
Self-kindness does not mean excusing harmful behavior or denying reality. It means responding without unnecessary cruelty toward yourself.
Speaking Shame
If your shame could speak, what would it say?
What would you say back to the shame that is honest, grounded, and protective of your dignity?
Guidance: You do not need to argue with every thought. You can notice the message, question whether it is fair or complete, and choose a response that does not attack your identity.

When to Stop
Stop the exercise if you notice that your distress is increasing, you feel overwhelmed, disconnected, unsafe, or unable to remain oriented to the present. You do not need to complete every section. Return to a grounding or stabilization practice that feels appropriate, contact a trusted support person, or seek professional help when needed.
