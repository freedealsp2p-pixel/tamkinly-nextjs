## 📖 Guide Specifications

### Page 1 — Title, Nervous System Overview, Trauma Responses
- Title: "Guide: What Trauma Does To The Body" — centered, large, Dark color
- Subtitle: "Understanding the Biological Basis of Trauma Responses" — smaller, Primary color
- Nervous system section:
  - Header: "Your Nervous System and Trauma" — Dark color
  - "Your autonomic nervous system has two main branches that regulate your body's state:"
  - "Sympathetic nervous system — activates the body for action (fight/flight). This is your accelerator. It increases heart rate, redirects blood to muscles, sharpens focus, and prepares you to respond to threat."
  - "Parasympathetic nervous system — calms the body for rest and recovery. This is your brake. It slows heart rate, promotes digestion, and supports healing and social engagement."
  - "Trauma disrupts the balance between these two branches. After trauma, the sympathetic system tends to stay overactive (hyperaroused) or the parasympathetic system applies an emergency shutdown (dissociation/freeze). The nervous system gets stuck in a protective mode that was helpful during the threat but continues after the threat has passed."
- Trauma responses summary (brief, referencing the four responses):
  - Header: "The Four Trauma Responses" — Dark color
  - "Fight — confronting the threat. Muscles tense, heart races, anger surges."
  - "Flight — escaping the threat. Restlessness, urge to run, anxiety peaks."
  - "Freeze — immobilizing. Body goes still, mind goes distant, voice goes quiet."
  - "Fawn — appeasing the threat. People-pleasing, compliance, self-abandonment."
  - "These responses are automatic, not chosen. They are controlled by brain structures that operate below conscious awareness — primarily the amygdala and brainstem."

### Page 2 — Window of Tolerance, Hypervigilance, Dissociation
- Window of tolerance section:
  - Header: "The Window of Tolerance" — Dark color
  - "The window of tolerance is the range of nervous system activation where you can think clearly, feel emotions without being overwhelmed, and respond flexibly to life. Within this window, you are functional and present."
  - "Above the window: Hyperarousal — you are too activated. Anxiety, panic, anger, emotional flooding, racing thoughts, inability to sit still."
  - "Below the window: Hypoarousal — you are too shut down. Numbness, dissociation, emptiness, flat affect, inability to think or move, feeling disconnected from your body."
  - "Trauma narrows the window. Things that would be manageable for someone without trauma history can push you above or below your window. The goal of trauma therapy is not to eliminate activation but to widen the window — so you can experience more without losing your capacity to function."
  - Visual: a horizontal bar/diagram showing the window with hyperarousal above and hypoarousal below, labeled clearly
- Hypervigilance section:
  - Header: "Hypervigilance" — Dark color
  - "Hypervigilance is a state of chronic threat scanning. Your nervous system stays on high alert, constantly checking for danger even in safe situations."
  - "Signs: difficulty relaxing, startle responses to minor sounds, scanning rooms for exits, trouble sleeping, interpreting neutral cues as threatening, physical tension that does not release"
  - "Hypervigilance is exhausting. It keeps your sympathetic nervous system engaged, which prevents the rest, digestion, and recovery that your body needs."
- Dissociation section:
  - Header: "Dissociation" — Dark color
  - "Dissociation is a protective mechanism that disconnects you from overwhelming experience. It exists on a spectrum:"
  - "Mild: daydreaming, highway hypnosis, losing track of time during a movie"
  - "Moderate: feeling foggy, emotionally numb, 'not really here,' watching yourself from outside"
  - "Severe: losing time, feeling like different parts of yourself, inability to remember significant events, feeling the world is not real (derealization) or you are not real (depersonalization)"
  - "Dissociation is not a choice. It is the parasympathetic nervous system's emergency override — a last-resort survival mechanism when fight and flight are not possible."

### Page 3 — Body-Based Responses, When to Seek Help
- Body-based responses section:
  - Header: "How Trauma Lives in the Body" — Dark color
  - "Trauma is not just a psychological experience — it is stored in the body. This is why talk therapy alone is sometimes not enough. The body holds what the mind cannot process."
  - "Common body-based trauma responses:"
  - "— Chronic muscle tension, especially in shoulders, jaw, and pelvis"
  - "— Digestive problems (IBS, nausea, appetite changes)"
  - "— Sleep disruption (insomnia, nightmares, night terrors)"
  - "— Chronic pain with no clear medical cause"
  - "— Autoimmune conditions and inflammatory responses"
  - "— Cardiovascular effects (elevated heart rate, blood pressure changes)"
  - "— Breathing pattern changes (shallow breathing, breath-holding, hyperventilation)"
  - "— Sexual difficulties"
  - "— Temperature regulation problems (always cold, sudden hot flashes)"
  - "— Fatigue and exhaustion that rest does not relieve"
  - "These are not 'all in your head.' They are the result of a nervous system that has been stuck in protective mode, flooding the body with stress hormones and preventing the restoration cycles that maintain health."
- When to seek help section:
  - Header: "When to Seek Professional Help" — Dark color
  - "Consider working with a trauma-informed professional if:"
  - "— Trauma responses are frequent and intense, interfering with daily life"
  - "— You cannot access a felt sense of safety, even in objectively safe situations"
  - "— Dissociation is severe or frequent (losing time, feeling unreal regularly)"
  - "— You are using substances, self-harm, or other strategies to manage overwhelm"
  - "— Relationships are being significantly affected"
  - "— You are having suicidal thoughts or feel you cannot keep yourself safe"
  - "— Body-based symptoms are chronic and unexplained by medical evaluation"
